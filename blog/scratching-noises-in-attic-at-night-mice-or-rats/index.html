Scratching Noises in the Attic at Night: Mice or Rats?

The sound alone will not tell you for certain what is up there, but a few details about timing, volume and location narrow it down quickly.

Does the sound itself tell you anything?

Rats are larger and heavier than mice, so their movement tends to sound like a distinct, deliberate scratching or thumping, sometimes with an audible weight behind each step. Mice produce a lighter, quicker scurrying sound, often described as scratching mixed with faster running. Neither description is exact, since insulation, duct work and the attic's construction all change how sound travels.

Gnawing is a useful clue on its own. A steady, rhythmic gnawing sound, especially if it continues for several minutes at a time, suggests an animal working on wood, wiring insulation or a wall stud, which both mice and rats do, though rats can chew through tougher material given their larger jaw strength.

Does the time of night matter?

Both mice and rats are nocturnal, so activity right after dark and again in the hours before dawn is typical for either animal. Roof rats in particular are known for being active climbers at night, and their travel along rafters or wall voids can sound like footsteps moving across the ceiling rather than a single stationary spot.

What other signs should I look for during the day?

Droppings are one of the clearest indicators, and size is the main difference: mouse droppings are small and pointed, roughly the size of a grain of rice, while rat droppings are noticeably larger and often blunt or rounded at the ends. Check near where you heard the noise, along the attic floor and near any stored boxes.

Grease marks along beams or joists, where an animal repeatedly brushes against the same surface, are another sign, more commonly associated with rats due to their oilier fur and heavier body contact. If you can safely access the attic, our rodent inspection service identifies the species definitively along with the entry points, which matters more for the fix than the sound alone.

Why does knowing the species actually matter?

Mice and rats use different entry points and different amounts of space to get in. A mouse can fit through a gap about the width of a pencil, while a rat typically needs an opening closer to the size of a quarter. If you seal for one species and it turns out to be the other, gaps that matter to the actual animal can go unaddressed.

Trap and bait placement also differs by species, since rats are often more cautious around new objects in their environment and may take longer to approach an unfamiliar trap. Our mouse control and rat control services use different approaches suited to each animal's typical behavior.

Could it be something other than a rodent?

Squirrels are a common attic visitor around Puyallup and can sound similar to rats, though squirrels are typically more active during the day than at night, with occasional early-morning or dusk activity. Raccoons produce heavier, more thumping sounds and are usually much louder than either mice or rats. Birds nesting in a vent or soffit can also create scratching sounds, though usually without the gnawing component.

If the sound pattern does not match typical rodent behavior, such as loud daytime activity or very heavy footsteps, mention that clearly when you call, since it may point toward a different kind of wildlife issue that needs a different approach.

Before you call.

  • Note whether the sound happens mostly at night or during the day.
  • Listen for whether it sounds like quick scurrying or heavier, deliberate movement.
  • If safely possible, check the attic during daylight for droppings or gnaw marks.
  • Do not attempt to access the attic if you are not comfortable doing so safely.

What if I only hear it occasionally, not every night?

Intermittent noise can still mean an active problem, since rodents don't necessarily move around the same amount every single night, and outdoor temperature or weather can affect how much time they spend in a warmer indoor space versus foraging outside. A quiet night doesn't mean the animal left, and several quiet nights in a row followed by renewed activity is a common pattern rather than something unusual.

If the noise has been going on intermittently for more than a couple of weeks, that's generally enough time for us to recommend an inspection rather than continuing to wait and see whether it resolves on its own.

Could there be more than one animal up there?

Yes, and this is common. Mice in particular breed quickly, so a single animal that got in a few weeks ago could already have a small litter established by the time you notice consistent noise. Multiple distinct sound sources, or noise coming from more than one part of the attic on different nights, can suggest more than one animal is present.

Rats are somewhat more territorial than mice, so multiple rats in a confined attic space is less automatic than with mice, but a breeding pair or small family group is still a realistic possibility if activity has gone unaddressed for a while. Either way, an inspection gives a more accurate sense of scale than trying to estimate from sound alone.

What happens once the species is confirmed?

Once we know whether it's mice, rats or something else, the plan follows a set order: identify entry points, remove active animals, seal the openings, and clean up any droppings or nest material left behind. If activity has been going on for a while, our attic and crawl space rodent cleanup service handles the mess safely, following CDC guidance for droppings that may carry pathogens.

Common questions

Can I tell mice from rats by sound alone over the phone?

Not with full certainty. Sound gives useful clues, like weight and speed of movement, but droppings and gnaw marks confirmed during an inspection are more reliable overall.

Is scratching at night always a rodent?

Usually, but not always. Squirrels, raccoons and nesting birds can all produce similar sounds. The timing and pattern help narrow it down, and an inspection confirms it.

How fast can rats or mice multiply once they're in the attic?

Both breed quickly. Mice can have several litters a year, and rats reproduce at a similar pace, so a small population can grow noticeably within just a few weeks if left alone.

Do I need to go into the attic myself before calling?

No, that's not required. Describe what you've heard and any daytime observations, and we'll bring the inspection to confirm the rest.

Is emergency service available for attic noise at night?

Yes, our line is open 24/7, and we schedule same-day visits when the calendar allows for that.

Does intermittent noise mean the problem is smaller than constant noise?

Not necessarily. Rodents don't move constantly, so quiet stretches don't confirm a smaller population, and an inspection is a more reliable way to judge scale than sound patterns alone.
