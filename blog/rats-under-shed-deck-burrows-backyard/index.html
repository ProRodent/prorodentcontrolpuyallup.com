Rats Under a Shed or Deck: Burrows in the Backyard

A shed or deck creates the exact combination of shelter and cover rats look for, which is why burrows so often show up right at the edge of these structures.

Why do rats burrow under sheds and decks specifically?

Norway rats, the species most commonly responsible for ground-level burrowing, prefer sheltered spots close to a food source. The space under a shed or deck offers protection from weather and predators while staying close to a yard, garden or compost area where food might be found. The structure above also hides the burrow entrance from casual view, letting a colony establish before anyone notices.

Loose or disturbed soil near a foundation edge is easier to dig into than compacted ground elsewhere in the yard, which is part of why burrows cluster right at the base of a structure rather than out in the open lawn.

What does a burrow actually look like?

A rat burrow entrance is typically two to four inches across, roughly round, and often has smoothed, worn edges from repeated use. Fresh burrows usually have loose dirt pushed out nearby, while an older or abandoned burrow may show some overgrowth or debris settling into the opening.

Active burrows often have more than one entrance, sometimes connected by tunnels a few feet apart, which is part of what makes a full burrow system harder to address than it first appears from the surface.

Is this always Norway rats specifically?

Ground-level burrowing is the classic Norway rat behavior, and it's the most likely explanation for a backyard burrow around Puyallup. Roof rats, by contrast, are better climbers and more often found nesting above ground, in trees, attics or dense vegetation, so a burrow at ground level points more toward Norway rats. Our post on Norway rats versus roof rats covers the distinction in more detail.

Why does this matter beyond just the yard?

A burrow system under a shed or deck is often a stepping stone toward the house itself, not an isolated backyard issue. Rats travel established paths, and a colony that starts outdoors can extend toward the home if food or shelter opportunities present themselves, particularly as outdoor conditions get wetter and colder.

Burrowing can also undermine the structural stability of a shed foundation or a deck footing over time, since repeated tunneling can create voids that shift how the structure settles. This is a separate concern from the rodent activity itself, but one worth addressing before it becomes a bigger repair.

How is a backyard burrow actually treated?

Treatment starts with confirming how extensive the burrow system is, since a single entrance can be misleading if it connects to a larger network. From there, treatment typically involves targeted trapping near active entrances and addressing the conditions that made the spot attractive in the first place, such as available food sources or excess vegetation providing cover.

Our backyard and outdoor rodent control service handles exactly this kind of ground-level activity, checking the full extent of a property rather than just the visible entrance. Rat control more broadly covers indoor and outdoor rat activity together when both are present.

What can I do to make my yard less attractive to burrowing?

Removing easy food sources makes the biggest difference: secure compost bins, clean up fallen fruit or bird seed, and keep pet food inside rather than left out overnight. Trimming back dense vegetation near a shed or deck removes some of the cover rats rely on to approach and dig unnoticed.

  • Secure compost in a container rats can't access rather than an open pile.
  • Clean up fallen fruit, nuts or bird seed regularly.
  • Store pet food indoors rather than leaving it outside overnight.
  • Trim back dense vegetation and ground cover near sheds and decks.
  • Check for and fill any small gaps at the base of a shed or deck skirting.

Should I try to fill in a burrow myself?

Filling in a burrow entrance without addressing the rats themselves usually just means the colony digs a new entrance nearby, since the underlying tunnel system and the rats using it are still there. It can also make it harder to assess the extent of the problem later, since a filled entrance hides how active the burrow actually was.

It's more effective to have the burrow assessed and treated first, and only then address the physical hole once you're confident the activity has stopped.

Does the type of shed or deck construction matter?

A shed or deck built directly on the ground, without a proper foundation or perimeter skirting, tends to be more vulnerable to burrowing than a raised structure with a solid concrete footing. There's simply less resistance to digging underneath a structure sitting right on bare soil. Adding a buried barrier, such as hardware cloth extending below grade around the perimeter of a shed, can make it significantly harder for rats to establish a burrow underneath in the first place.

If a deck or shed already shows burrow activity, our rodent exclusion service can install this kind of below-grade barrier as part of a broader plan, rather than just treating the visible burrow entrance and leaving the underlying vulnerability unaddressed for the future.

If you find a burrow in your yard.

  • Note the location and how many entrances you can see.
  • Do not fill in the burrow before it's been assessed.
  • Check nearby for droppings, gnaw marks or a travel path toward the house.
  • Call and describe what you found so we can plan a full inspection.

Common questions

Are backyard rat burrows dangerous to my house's foundation?

Burrowing near a shed or deck can undermine soil stability around that structure over time. Whether it affects the main house depends on proximity, so an inspection can assess the actual risk to your property.

How many rats are usually in one burrow system?

It varies widely, from a couple of animals to a larger colony with multiple connected tunnels. The visible entrances alone don't tell you the full number.

Can I use smoke bombs or water to flush out a burrow myself?

We don't recommend DIY flushing methods, since they can be unpredictable and may not address the full tunnel system. A professional assessment identifies the actual extent before any treatment begins.

Will removing food sources alone solve the problem?

It helps prevent new activity, but an already-established burrow with rats living in it typically needs direct treatment, not just removing what attracted them originally.

Is this more common in certain seasons around Puyallup?

Burrowing activity can happen year-round, but it often becomes more noticeable as outdoor conditions get wetter and rats seek out drier, more sheltered ground closer to structures.

Does a raised deck reduce burrowing risk compared to one built low to the ground?

Generally yes, since more clearance and a solid footing give rats less room and less resistance for digging underneath compared to a structure sitting directly on soil.
