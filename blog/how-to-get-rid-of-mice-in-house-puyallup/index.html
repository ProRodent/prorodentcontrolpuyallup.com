How to Get Rid of Mice in Your House Near Puyallup

Mice keep coming back when only part of the problem gets addressed, so the order of the work matters as much as the work itself.

Why does the order of steps matter?

A common mistake is sealing entry points before removing the mice already inside, which can trap animals in a wall where they die and create an odor problem, or lead them to chew a new way out somewhere else. The reverse mistake, trapping without ever sealing anything, means new mice can simply replace the ones caught, since the entry point that let the first group in is still open.

The order that actually works is: find where they're getting in, remove the ones already inside, then seal the entry points. Cleanup of droppings and any repair work comes after that, once the situation is stable.

How do I find where mice are getting in?

Mice leave a trail if you know where to look. Grease marks along baseboards, chewed material near a gap, and droppings clustered near a specific spot all point toward a nearby entry point. Check around utility penetrations, garage door thresholds, foundation vents and any place a pipe or cable enters the house.

A thorough check often finds more than one gap, since mice will use whatever opening is easiest, and a house can have several small openings that each need attention. Our rodent inspection service covers the exterior and interior systematically, which catches gaps that are easy to miss from a quick look around.

Should I use traps, bait, or both?

Traps are generally the more controllable option for indoor use, since you know exactly where the animal is once caught, and there's no risk of a mouse dying somewhere inaccessible after eating bait. Snap traps and multiple-catch traps both work when placed correctly, along walls where mice actually travel rather than out in open floor space.

Bait stations have a place, particularly for ongoing outdoor monitoring around a structure's perimeter, but placement and safety around children and pets matter, and tamper-resistant stations are worth the extra cost. Our rodent trapping and removal service places whichever combination fits your situation and explains the reasoning.

How long does it typically take to clear an infestation?

A light, early problem, caught within the first week or two of activity, might clear up within a week of proper trapping and sealing. A more established population, with multiple generations already breeding indoors, can take several weeks of trapping combined with sealing before activity stops completely.

Follow-up matters here. A visit or two after the initial work confirms whether the traps are still catching anything and whether the seal is holding, rather than assuming the first round of work solved everything.

What about cleaning up after they're gone?

Droppings and urine can carry pathogens, so cleanup should follow CDC guidance rather than just sweeping the area. Ventilate the space, avoid stirring up dust from dry droppings, and disinfect surfaces before wiping them down. Our post on cleaning up mouse droppings safely walks through the specific steps.

If activity has been going on for a while in an attic or crawl space, insulation may be contaminated enough that attic and crawl space rodent cleanup makes more sense than a DIY approach, especially if the space is hard to access safely.

When should I call instead of handling it myself?

If you've placed traps for more than a week or two without a clear reduction in activity, that usually means the entry point hasn't been found, or there are more mice than the trap count suggests. A professional inspection at that point often finds what a DIY search missed.

Homes with hard-to-access crawl spaces, extensive activity across multiple rooms, or a strong odor suggesting a dead animal in a wall are also good reasons to call rather than continue on your own.

What about DIY natural or home remedies?

Peppermint oil, dryer sheets and ultrasonic plug-in devices are commonly suggested online as mouse deterrents, and while some people report short-term results, there isn't strong, consistent evidence that any of these reliably keeps mice away on their own. A hungry mouse with limited other options will generally push past a smell it finds unpleasant if the alternative is going without food or shelter.

We're not going to tell you these approaches never work at all. But we don't rely on them as a primary strategy, since finding and sealing the actual entry point addresses the root cause, rather than trying to make the house generally less appealing while gaps remain wide open.

What if I have pets that might interfere with traps?

Snap traps and bait stations both carry some risk around curious pets, particularly dogs that might investigate a trap or cats that could be exposed to bait. Enclosed, tamper-resistant bait stations reduce this risk significantly, and trap placement in areas pets can't access, such as behind appliances or inside cabinets with restricted access, is another practical option.

Tell us about any pets in the home when you call, since we plan placement around where they spend time, and we can discuss which trap types make the most sense for a household with animals present.

A practical starting checklist.

  • Walk the exterior looking for gaps around utilities, vents and the garage door.
  • Place traps along walls near where you've seen droppings or heard activity.
  • Do not seal any gap until you're confident the mice inside are cleared.
  • Clean droppings following CDC guidance, not by sweeping or vacuuming dry.
  • Call if traps aren't working after a week or two, or if the space is hard to access.

Common questions

Can I get rid of mice with just store-bought traps?

Sometimes, for a small, early problem. But if you can't find the entry point, new mice tend to replace the ones you catch, so sealing matters as much as trapping.

Is it safe to use poison instead of traps?

Poison carries risk of secondary poisoning to pets or wildlife that eat a poisoned mouse, and a mouse can also die somewhere inaccessible, causing an odor problem. Traps give you more control over the outcome.

How do I know if the mice are really gone?

A stretch of time with no new droppings, no trap catches, and no sounds is the best indicator, though a follow-up inspection confirms it more reliably than guessing.

Do I need to seal every gap in my house?

You need to seal the gaps mice are actually using, which an inspection identifies. Sealing everything imaginable isn't necessary if the real entry points are addressed.

Is same-day service available in the Puyallup area?

Often, yes, depending on the day's schedule. Call and describe what you're seeing, and we'll tell you the earliest available time for a visit.

Do peppermint oil or ultrasonic devices actually keep mice out?

There isn't strong evidence either reliably works on its own. We treat sealing entry points as the primary strategy rather than relying on deterrents alone.
