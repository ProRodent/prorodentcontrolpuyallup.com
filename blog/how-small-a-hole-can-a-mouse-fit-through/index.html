How Small a Hole Can a Mouse Fit Through?

A mouse's skeleton is more flexible than most people expect, and its skull, not its body, is usually the limiting factor for how small a gap it can use.

What's the actual size a mouse needs?

A house mouse can squeeze through an opening about the diameter of a pencil, roughly a quarter inch across. Their skull is the narrowest rigid part of their body, and if the skull fits, the rest of the body can typically compress and follow through the same gap. That's a smaller opening than most people picture when they imagine an entry point.

Young mice can fit through even smaller gaps than adults, which means a home that seems mouse-proof to an adult mouse might still let in a juvenile. This is part of why an inspection focused on obvious large gaps can still miss the actual entry point.

How does that compare to rats?

Rats need a larger opening, generally around the size of a quarter, since their skull and body are proportionally larger even though rats are also flexible climbers and squeezers. Norway rats, more common at ground level, often use burrow-sized gaps near foundations, while roof rats are known for accessing homes through gaps higher up, near rooflines and eaves.

Where do these small gaps usually hide?

Utility penetrations are a common culprit: the point where a pipe, cable or dryer vent passes through an exterior wall often has a gap around it that was never fully sealed, either during original construction or after a later repair. These gaps can be easy to overlook since they're often behind landscaping or in a less-visited part of the exterior.

Garage door thresholds are another frequent entry point, since worn or gapped weatherstripping along the bottom edge can leave an opening wide enough for a mouse even when the door looks fully closed. Foundation vents with damaged or missing screens, and settled cracks where a foundation has shifted slightly over time, round out the most common spots we find during an inspection.

  • Gaps around pipes, cables and dryer vents where they pass through an exterior wall.
  • Worn or gapped weatherstripping along garage door thresholds.
  • Foundation or crawl space vents with damaged or missing screens.
  • Settled cracks in a foundation or where an addition meets the original structure.
  • Gaps at rooflines, soffits or where a tree branch touches the roof.

Why do newer homes still have these gaps?

It's a common assumption that new construction means a tightly sealed home, but builders often leave small gaps around utility penetrations that were never a priority to seal completely, since they don't affect the home's function or appearance. These gaps are just as usable to a mouse as an older home's settled crack, and in some cases a newer subdivision on the plateau can see just as much activity as an older in-town neighborhood.

Renovations and additions can introduce new gaps too, particularly where new plumbing or electrical work meets an older wall, since the seal between old and new materials isn't always completed with rodent-proofing in mind.

How do you actually seal a gap this small?

Foam is a common DIY choice, but mice can chew through foam fairly easily, so it tends to fail within a year or two, especially outdoors where it's exposed to moisture and temperature swings. Materials like hardware cloth, sheet metal flashing and mortar are more durable choices, since a mouse's teeth can't get through them the way they can through foam or plastic.

Our rodent exclusion service uses these more durable materials specifically because a seal that fails within a year means paying for the same work twice. Finding every gap on a property, not just the obvious ones, is the part that benefits most from a trained eye during an inspection.

Can I just check for gaps myself?

You can do a basic walk-around, and it's worth doing. Walk the entire exterior slowly, checking at ground level and looking up toward the roofline, paying attention to any spot where two different materials meet, such as where siding meets the foundation or where a pipe passes through a wall.

That said, a thorough inspection catches gaps that are easy to miss from a casual look, particularly ones behind landscaping, under a deck, or up near a roofline that's hard to see from the ground. Our rodent inspection service covers both the exterior and interior systematically.

What if I've already found and closed a few gaps?

Closing a few visible gaps is a reasonable start, but it doesn't mean the problem is fully solved if mice were already inside before the sealing happened. If activity continues after sealing what seemed like the obvious entry points, that usually means either a gap was missed, or the mice sealed inside are now finding a different way out, sometimes chewing a brand new opening in the process.

This is why our mouse control service pairs trapping with exclusion rather than treating sealing alone as the complete answer. Confirming the animals inside are cleared, and then verifying no new activity appears after sealing, gives a more reliable result than sealing and hoping for the best.

Does the material used for sealing matter as much as finding the gap?

Yes, and this trips people up often. Finding every entry point matters, but using a material a mouse can chew through defeats the purpose just as much as missing the gap entirely. A mouse's front teeth grow continuously and are strong enough to chew through foam, plastic, thin wood and even some soft metals given enough time and motivation.

Hardware cloth, heavier gauge sheet metal, and mortar for masonry gaps resist chewing in a way that softer materials simply don't. That's why our rodent exclusion service relies on these materials rather than a quick foam application that looks finished but doesn't actually hold up over time.

A quick self-check you can do today.

  • Walk the full exterior perimeter slowly, checking ground level first.
  • Look at every spot where a pipe, cable or vent passes through a wall.
  • Check garage door weatherstripping for gaps or wear.
  • Look up at the roofline and eaves for visible gaps.
  • Note anything you find, even if you're not sure it's a real entry point.

Common questions

Can a mouse really fit through something that small?

Yes. A mouse's flexible skeleton allows it to compress its body to fit through an opening about the width of a pencil, as long as its skull can pass through.

If I seal the big gaps, is that usually enough?

Not always. The gaps mice actually use are often the small, easy-to-miss ones, not the obviously large openings people notice first.

Does steel wool work to plug a small gap?

Steel wool can be a temporary measure in a pinch, but it degrades over time, especially with moisture exposure. A more permanent seal uses materials like hardware cloth or sheet metal.

Do I need a full inspection if I've only seen one mouse?

One mouse can mean an isolated visitor, or it can mean more are already inside. An inspection helps determine which situation you're actually dealing with.

Is exclusion work something I can do myself?

Some smaller gaps are manageable for a confident DIYer with the right materials, but finding every entry point and sealing durably against chewing is where professional work tends to hold up better long term.
