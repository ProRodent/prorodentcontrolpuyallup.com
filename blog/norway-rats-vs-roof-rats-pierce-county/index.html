Norway Rats vs. Roof Rats in Pierce County

Both species turn up around Pierce County, but they use a property very differently, which changes where you should look and how a problem gets treated.

What's the basic difference between the two?

Norway rats are the larger, stockier of the two, with a blunter nose and a tail shorter than their body length. They are strong burrowers and tend to stay at ground level, favoring foundations, burrows in yards, and lower parts of a structure like a crawl space or basement.

Roof rats are somewhat smaller and more slender, with a pointed nose and a tail longer than their body. As the name suggests, they are excellent climbers, and they're more often found in attics, upper walls, trees and dense vegetation than at ground level.

Which species is more common around Pierce County?

Both species are present in the Puget Sound region, and which one you're dealing with often comes down to the specific property and its surroundings. A home near dense tree cover, wooded lots or with overhanging branches close to the roof is more likely to see roof rat activity, while a property with more open ground, gardens or nearby drainage features tends to see more Norway rat activity.

Properties in Puyallup's more rural, wooded areas, like those near Graham or the Carbon River foothills, can see either species depending on the immediate landscape around the structure, so location alone doesn't fully predict which one you have.

How does the difference affect where I should look for signs?

If you suspect Norway rats, check the yard first: look for burrow entrances near a shed, deck or foundation edge, and check basement or crawl space areas for droppings and gnaw marks at ground level. Our post on rat burrows under a shed or deck covers what these ground-level signs look like in detail.

If you suspect roof rats, focus on the attic, upper walls and areas near where trees or vegetation touch the roofline. Scratching sounds specifically in the ceiling rather than in a basement or ground-floor wall lean toward roof rats given their climbing habits.

Does the species change how treatment works?

Trap and bait placement differ by species since they use different parts of a property. Ground-level trapping near burrows and foundation gaps addresses Norway rats, while roof rat treatment often focuses on attic access points, rooflines and any vegetation providing a bridge to the structure.

Exclusion work also differs in focus: Norway rat prevention centers on foundation and ground-level gaps, while roof rat prevention often includes trimming back tree branches near the roof and sealing gaps at the roofline itself. Our rat control service adjusts the approach based on which species the inspection identifies.

Can both species be present on the same property?

Yes, and it's not unusual, particularly on larger properties with both open ground and substantial tree cover. A thorough inspection checks both ground-level and upper-level signs rather than assuming a single species based on one initial observation.

If activity is found in both an attic and a backyard burrow, treating only one location typically means the other continues undisturbed, so a full-property approach matters more than treating whichever sign was noticed first by whoever spotted it.

How does entry-point sealing differ between the two?

Both species need an opening roughly the size of a quarter to get through, but the location of that opening differs. Norway rats look for ground-level gaps, while roof rats look for openings near the roofline, soffits, or gaps where utility lines or tree branches provide access higher up. Our rodent exclusion service addresses both types of gaps depending on what an inspection identifies.

How does treatment differ if activity is already established indoors?

Once either species is inside a living space rather than just an attic or crawl void, the approach shifts toward locating the specific rooms or areas affected and placing traps accordingly. Norway rats indoors tend to stay low, near baseboards, under appliances or inside cabinets, while roof rats indoors are more likely to travel through ceiling voids and show up unexpectedly in an upper room, sometimes a fair distance from where they first entered the structure.

Our rodent trapping and removal service adjusts trap placement to match these different travel patterns rather than using a one-size-fits-all approach regardless of which species is actually present in the home.

Does one species cause more structural damage than the other?

Both species share the same continuously growing incisors and gnaw on wood, wiring and insulation as a normal behavior, so neither is inherently less destructive than the other. The difference tends to be about location: Norway rat damage often shows up near foundations, ground-level framing, or a basement, while roof rat damage more often affects attic insulation, roof framing and wiring that runs through upper parts of a structure.

If chewing has already caused damage to insulation, wiring or wood framing, our rodent damage repair service addresses that once the animals are cleared and the entry points identified during inspection are sealed properly. We evaluate the extent of the damage during the same visit that confirms which species was responsible, so repair planning and treatment can move forward together rather than as two disconnected steps handled weeks apart.

Quick signs to help identify which species you have.

  • Ground-level burrows or gnaw marks near a foundation suggest Norway rats.
  • Scratching or droppings specifically in an attic suggest roof rats.
  • A shorter, thicker tail relative to body length suggests Norway rat.
  • Activity near tree branches touching the roof suggests roof rats.
  • Both signs present may mean both species are active on the property.

Common questions

Are roof rats actually common in Washington state?

Yes, roof rats are present in parts of Western Washington, particularly in areas with dense tree cover, though Norway rats are also common and the two species can overlap on the same property.

Is one species more dangerous than the other?

We don't make health-risk comparisons between species. Both can cause structural damage and contaminate areas with droppings, so either warrants the same careful treatment and cleanup approach.

Can I tell the species apart just from droppings, or do I need more?

Droppings alone give a general size clue but aren't always definitive on their own. Location, travel pattern and physical sightings together give a more reliable answer than droppings alone would.

Should I trim trees near my roof to prevent roof rats?

Yes, branches touching or overhanging a roofline give roof rats an easy bridge onto the structure, so trimming that back is a reasonable prevention step.

Can Norway rats climb at all, even though they prefer the ground?

They can climb to some degree, particularly short vertical distances or rough surfaces, but they're far less agile and far less likely to nest above ground than roof rats, which are true climbing specialists built for it.

Does an inspection identify the species before treatment starts?

Yes, our inspection process specifically looks for species-identifying signs like droppings, gnaw marks, grease trails and travel patterns before we finalize any treatment plan at all for your specific property.
