Why the yard matters as much as the house.

By the time rodents are inside a home, they have usually been active in the yard for a while first. Burrow holes along a fence line, a compost pile that never gets fully covered, or a bird feeder that scatters seed onto the ground all give rodents food and cover close to a foundation. Addressing that outdoor activity is often the difference between a problem that stays outside and one that moves in.

Puyallup yards vary from tight in-town lots downtown to larger properties on the edges of South Hill, but the same features draw rodents in either setting: dense shrubs against the foundation, wood piles, sheds with gaps underneath, and blackberry brambles along a back fence.

What we look for outdoors.

  • Burrow entrances along fence lines, under sheds, or in dense ground cover.
  • Gnaw marks on shed siding, deck framing or stored outdoor items.
  • Compost areas, fallen fruit, or bird feeder overspray that provide a steady food source.
  • Wood piles, dense shrubs and blackberry brambles that give rodents cover close to the house.
  • Drainage ditches or low, damp areas that hold ground moisture rats prefer for burrowing.

We do not expect every yard to be cleared of all these features, since some, like an established garden bed or a wood pile you rely on, are not going anywhere. What we do is treat the active burrows and travel routes directly and tell you honestly which features are contributing the most, so you can decide what to change.

Treating burrows and outdoor nests.

An active burrow often has more than one entrance, so we look for the full extent of the system rather than treating a single visible hole. Traps and stations go on the routes between burrow entrances and food or water sources, and we do not recommend flooding or filling burrows on your own before an inspection, since that can just push rats to dig a new entrance nearby.

If burrow activity has undermined a shed floor, deck footing or fence post, we flag that structural concern separately, since repairing settled ground or damaged framing is outside a rodent control visit but worth knowing about.

Sheds and detached structures.

Sheds are a common overlooked entry point, since a gap under a shed floor or a torn vent screen rarely gets the same attention as the main house. We check shed exteriors and interiors as part of a full backyard visit. If a shed is being used to store food, pet supplies or garden feed, that is worth mentioning when you call, since it can be a significant draw on its own. Where a shed gap connects to the same yard area as a garage, we look at both together rather than treating one and leaving the other. Our garage and basement rodent control service covers the attached structure side of that same problem.

Once outdoor activity is under control, the next question is usually whether the house itself has any gaps that outdoor rodents could still use to get inside. A yard visit on its own does not seal a foundation crack or a crawl space vent. For a property with both outdoor activity and a history of indoor sightings, we usually recommend pairing this visit with our rodent exclusion service, so the two problems are not treated as separate jobs when they are really one.

Outdoor rodent control through the seasons.

Outdoor activity in the Puyallup area does not stop in winter the way it might in a colder climate. Rats and mice stay active in yards through the wet season, though they push harder toward structures as the ground gets consistently wet and cold. Spring and summer bring different pressures, including fallen fruit from garden trees and heavier plant growth that gives extra cover.

South Hill properties with larger, more established landscaping sometimes need a longer initial walk than a tighter in-town lot, simply because there is more ground to cover. We do not shorten that walk to save time, since a missed burrow or overlooked bramble patch just means a callback later.

Before we arrive.

  • Note where you have seen burrow holes, droppings or gnaw marks in the yard.
  • Do not fill in burrow entrances or flood them with water before the visit.
  • Keep pets away from any area with a suspected active burrow.
  • Mention any compost, feeders or wood piles near the affected area.

Questions about backyard and outdoor rodent control

Can you get rid of rats living in my yard without them coming into the house?

Often, yes. Treating burrows and travel routes outdoors, combined with sealing any foundation gaps as prevention, is usually enough to keep an outdoor problem from becoming an indoor one.

Should I stop feeding birds if I have rat problems?

You do not necessarily need to stop, but using a tray to catch spilled seed and cleaning up underneath the feeder regularly removes a steady food source that rats rely on.

Is it safe to fill in a burrow myself?

We recommend against it before an inspection, since a burrow often has more than one entrance, and filling one without treating the rats inside usually just leads to a new entrance appearing nearby.

Do blackberry brambles really make a difference?

Yes, dense bramble growth along a fence line gives rats a protected corridor and sometimes a food source. Cutting growth back from the fence where practical reduces that cover.

Can you treat a shed separately from the main house?

Yes, sheds are included in a full backyard visit, and we check them for the same signs of activity as the yard and the house.

Does outdoor rodent activity slow down in winter here?

Not as much as in colder climates. Rats and mice stay active through the wet season in western Washington, though they push harder toward buildings as the ground gets consistently wet.
